# design-economy: the reasons behind it

This holds the reasons for `design-economy.md`, moved from the shared notes file.

The reasons behind `design-economy.md`, from the user, 2026-11-14, after a survey of the code's size found under 5% of hand-written production to cut with the design as it is.

- **The design first:** "in principle one could try to 'refactor' the design first, to see if the same functionality/model/flexibility could be expressed more economically, with fewer concept and a more elegant/simpler/orthogonal design? that would actually be the simplification that drives code reduction."
- **How much detail the implementer needs:** "One of the reasons of the design as contract is that design is produced by claude opus 5.5 (more capable desiger, better taste) and code by codex sol 6.1 (efficient coder, once given detailed instructions via a design like that). It would be interesting to know how much detail could the design lose without codex becoming sloppy and producing bad code, iow how much we need to hand hold codex via design." Hence detail on demand (`design-economy.md`, 6) and its list as the pilot's measure (`design-economy.md`, 7.3).
- **What the cost is:** "Cost for me is not so much tokens, but rather code complexity. What I want is good well organized quality code, because I think it's the basis for sustained growth, service stability and quality of the product in general."
- **Where it runs:** "keep execution of your experiment in temper" and "use the same repo": the pilot's goals land in main, from a template of their own (`design-economy.md`, 7 and 8), with no fork and no second tree.
- **Its execution:** "You can start with the draft. After you complete it, you'll contact temper/dev or ops to create the new template for the modified charters, and you'll hand the draft to temper/queue for starting execution."
- **A review round,** one, on Codex: its seven findings taken. The contracts between a goal's changes stay in the design, in a document of the goal's own that its last change deletes (4.3), rather than in the specs, so the charters' rules on contracts and their revision hold. E1 brings the delegation, rules and room the copied charters need, built from the text they resolve to on the deployment. Test worlds adapt to the internal types that go, with their cases and oracles unchanged (7.2). The measures are read at two fixed endpoints, with moves counted where they land and guidance counted across design, contracts and specs; the changes' records are collected after landing, since a change that lands without a question never reaches the designer (7.3; E3).
- **The draft's readings,** not the user's words: the design's detail traced partly to the stock designer charter's contracts between changes (`design-economy.md`, 3), kept for the goal's duration only (4.3); lean specs, since the spec is most of what the implementer reads and the user's question is how much it needs (6); what a design keeps and leaves (4); the concept budget (5); the pilot's rules carried by the draft and its template, not by `DESIGN.md`, until adoption (9); the area picked by a read-only survey (7.1).
- **The second pilot,** 2026-11-14, after E3's count: "'code that's clear and cheap to change' is surely a much more valuable goal than code size alone. If code reduction comes from it as a plus, that's good, if not we'll leave with that, but at least we have sane code." Its go: "ok, go for it, pick your recommendations for 'Yours to decide' and we'll review the outcome when the pilot is done."
- **The picks left to the draft** (`one-owner.md`): hearth's D013 reopened, render's records first, then configuration; the model's vocabularies written by the pilot's goal, not at adoption, since both crates that copy render's values are machines, which by the model's letter may name no domain's types; `reference-code-production.notes.md`, section 6's figures, with success read from the count.
